Engagement rate is the number everyone quotes and almost nobody defines. It is genuinely useful,
but only once you know what it is measuring and what it quietly leaves out.

## There is no standard formula

The first problem is that "engagement rate" is not one metric. Depending on who is calculating it,
you might be looking at:

- Likes and comments divided by **followers**
- Likes, comments, saves and shares divided by **followers**
- Any of the above divided by **reach** or by **impressions**

These produce very different numbers for the same account. An engagement rate calculated against
reach will almost always look healthier than one calculated against followers, because reach
excludes the followers who never saw the post.

So the first question about any engagement figure is not "is it good" but "what is the
denominator". A creator quoting 12% and a tool reporting 3% for the same account may both be
correct.

## Why a high rate can be a bad sign

Engagement rate correlates inversely with audience size, and that is normal. Smaller accounts have
audiences that know them; large accounts accumulate passive followers. A 9% rate on a 5,000-follower
account and a 2% rate on a 500,000-follower account can represent similar audience quality.

A genuinely high rate on a large account is worth a second look, not immediate excitement. Things
that inflate it:

- **Giveaway posts**, which attract comments from people with no interest in the product
- **Comment pods**, where groups reciprocate engagement regardless of content
- **Purchased engagement**, which is cheap and increasingly hard to spot from the outside
- **A single viral post** dragging an otherwise flat average upward

None of these are proof of anything on their own. They are reasons to look at the distribution
rather than the average.

> An average hides the thing you need to see. Ask for the spread.

## What to look at instead

Engagement rate is a useful filter and a poor decision-making tool. Once a creator passes the
filter, these tell you considerably more.

**Comment quality.** Read thirty of them. Real audiences ask specific questions and reference
things from earlier posts. Pods and bought engagement produce generic approval - emoji strings,
"nice", "great post".

**Saves and shares.** Saves indicate genuine intent, and shares indicate the content was worth
someone's own reputation. Both are far harder to fake than likes and correlate much better with
purchase behaviour.

**Consistency across recent posts.** Ten recent posts with similar performance describes a reliable
audience. Nine flat posts and one enormous spike describes a creator who got lucky once.

**Audience overlap with your customer.** A fitness creator in Pune with a 7% rate is irrelevant if
you only ship to Delhi NCR. Location, age band and language decide whether the engagement is
reachable at all.

**Past collaborations in your category.** Someone who has run brand work before knows how briefs,
approvals and disclosure work. That is worth real money in coordination time saved.

## How we treat it

On WebRectangle, engagement rate is the first metric on every creator card, ahead of follower
count. That ordering is deliberate: the product exists on the premise that relevance beats reach,
and putting follower count first would contradict it.

But it is a starting filter, not a verdict. Where the data supports it we surface **signals** -
patterns worth a closer look. We do not label creators as fraudulent, because no data available
from outside a platform's own systems supports that claim with any confidence, and anyone telling
you otherwise is selling something.

The number narrows the field. Reading the comments makes the decision.
