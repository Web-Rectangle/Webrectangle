Most disappointing collaborations were decided before a camera was switched on. The content was
never the problem. The brief was.

A bad brief usually fails in one of two directions: it says almost nothing, or it says far too much
about the wrong things. Both produce the same outcome, which is content that technically satisfies
the ask and lands flat.

## What a brief actually needs

There are six things a creator needs in order to give you a real answer and then make something
good. Everything else is optional.

- **The product, honestly.** What it is, who it is for, and what it costs. If there is a weakness,
  say so - a creator who discovers it mid-shoot will either stop or work around it badly.
- **The objective.** Awareness, a launch, sales, or content you intend to reuse. These produce
  genuinely different videos, and "all of them" produces none of them.
- **The audience you are actually after.** Not your entire addressable market. The specific slice
  this campaign is for.
- **Deliverables and timeline.** Two Reels and three Stories over three weeks. Not "some content,
  soon".
- **Budget.** Withholding it to see what they quote wastes a round of messages and starts the
  relationship badly.
- **Usage rights.** Whether you intend to run this as an ad, for how long, and on which platforms.
  This materially changes the price, and discovering it after the fact is the single most common
  source of collaboration disputes.

## What to leave out

Here is the uncomfortable part: the more precisely you script it, the worse it usually performs.

A creator's audience follows them because of how they talk. A brand-written script overrides
exactly the thing you are paying for. The result is an ad that happens to have a familiar face in
it, and audiences are extremely good at spotting those.

Give the non-negotiables - the claims that must appear, the claims that legally must not, the
disclosure requirement - and hand the rest over.

> Brief the outcome and the constraints. Do not brief the sentences.

## Non-negotiables are different from preferences

Be explicit about which is which. "The product must be shown in use" is a requirement. "We would
prefer a bright kitchen" is a preference. If you present both with the same weight, a creator will
treat them both as requirements and lose the room they needed to make it work.

A short list of genuine requirements is far more useful than a long list of mixed instructions.

## Say how it will be judged

If you are going to evaluate this on saves and comments, say that in the brief. A creator
optimising for reach makes a different video from one optimising for saves, and they will happily
make the one you actually want - if they know which it is.

This is also the moment to be realistic. A single collaboration with a mid-tier creator is not
going to move quarterly revenue, and setting that as the stated bar guarantees everyone involved
records a failure.

## Revisions, agreed up front

State how many rounds are included and what counts as a revision. "Move the product two seconds
earlier" is a revision. "Reshoot it in a different location" is a new shoot. Agreeing the
difference before anyone films saves an argument that always costs more than the clarity would
have.

## The test

Before sending a brief, read it as though you were the creator, and ask whether you could give a
firm yes or no without asking anything back.

If you could not, it is not finished. Every question a creator has to ask is a day added to the
timeline and a small deduction from how seriously they take the rest of it.
